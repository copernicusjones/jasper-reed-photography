How to Price Your First Photography Session (and Actually Get Paid for It)

Photography Business Resources · Free Guide

The biggest mistake new photographers make: setting a price based on what other beginners charge, or worse, guessing. Here's how to set a rate that's fair, covers your costs, and doesn't leave money on the table.

1. Calculate Your Break-Even Rate

Before you set a single price, know what it costs you to shoot one session. Add up:

  • Your time (pre-call, shoot, editing, delivery) — use an hourly rate, even if it's low
  • Software subscriptions (editing, gallery hosting, accounting)
  • Hardware amortization (camera, lenses, lights — spread over expected lifespan)
  • Insurance, website, marketing, business overhead

Break-even = your cost per session. Anything below that is you paying to work.

2. Pick a Pricing Model

  • Hourly: simple, but clients focus on time not value
  • Per-Project / Session Fee: covers your base cost; add a la carte options
  • Package: 2–3 tiers (basic / standard / premium) — easiest for clients to compare

3. Anchor Against the Market, Not Your Neighbors

Look at photographers in your market with similar output quality — not the beginners in your Facebook group. A session in a mid-size city with 2 edits + digital rights runs differently than in a major metro.

4. Add a Buffer for First-Year Learning

Your first 10–20 shoots take longer than you think. Build 20–30% extra time into your rate. You will underprice — plan for it.

5. Require a Deposit + Clear Payment Terms

50% deposit to hold the date, balance due before delivery of finals. Late fees after 14 days. Payment terms in writing, on every invoice, every time. No verbal "I'll send it this week."
